## 02. Primitive Quantities and Base Notation

This section establishes the minimal primitive object set required for CIITR to function as a boundary apparatus rather than a rhetorical label. The intent is definitional stability. The primitives defined here SHALL be treated as irreducible within the kernel, meaning they are not to be reinterpreted as aliases for adjacent constructs such as generic information, generic feedback, generic complexity, or generic dynamical recurrence. Any such reinterpretation constitutes a scope violation under Section 01.

### 2.1 System $S$, states, and representational manifold $M_S$ (if invoked)

A **system** $S$ denotes the bounded evaluation target to which CIITR claims are attached. $S$ is not a philosophical subject, and not a narrative agent. It is an object of protocol-bound classification.

A system is treated as admitting a state description indexed by time:

$$
S \;\mapsto\; x_S(t)
$$

where $x_S(t)$ denotes a state proxy sufficient for defining observables and temporal binding. The kernel does not constrain how $x_S(t)$ is parameterized. It only constrains that the parameterization be stable enough to support protocol-level observability and disqualification logic.

If the analysis requires an internal representational space, CIITR permits introduction of a **system-specific representational manifold** $M_S$ as an auxiliary primitive. $M_S$ is optional and SHALL only be invoked when its presence is necessary to preserve definitional coherence of $\Phi_i$ and $R_g$ in a given formalization. When invoked, $M_S$ SHALL not be treated as a claim about phenomenology or about a specific architecture. It is a formal placeholder denoting that the system admits an internal relational organization over which integration and re-entry can be meaningfully defined.

Accordingly:

- $M_S$ is a *semantic support object*, not an empirical assertion.
- If $M_S$ is introduced, all downstream uses SHALL specify whether a statement is about the manifold level or only about observable signatures.

### 2.2 Primitive axes: $\Phi_i$ and $R_g$

CIITR fixes two primitive axes. They are primitive in the kernel sense: they are not defined as composites of other CIITR terms, and they cannot be replaced by surrogate constructs without breaking the boundary logic.

#### 2.2.1 Integrated relational information $\Phi_i$

$\Phi_i$ denotes **integrated relational information**, and SHALL be treated as a structured integration predicate, not as "information" in the generic sense. The kernel binding is negative as well as positive: $\Phi_i$ is explicitly not reducible to raw data, token count, entropy in the abstract, storage volume, or undifferentiated complexity.

At kernel level, $\Phi_i$ is introduced as a primitive axis whose admissible observables must track *relational binding across elements*, rather than isolated local associations. The specific operationalization is deferred to the instrumentation chapter, but the semantic boundary is fixed here: a procedure that can only witness local correlation, local consistency, or local coherence SHALL not be allowed to count as direct evidence of $\Phi_i$ unless it also satisfies an explicit integration admissibility rule declared under protocol $\Pi$.

#### 2.2.2 Rhythmic epistemic re-entry $R_g$

$R_g$ denotes **rhythmic epistemic re-entry**, and SHALL be treated as an epistemic class, not as generic feedback. The term "epistemic" is binding: it asserts that the re-entry is constrained by internal consistency pressures, self-referential integrity constraints, or other kernel-compatible mechanisms that function as endogenous restriction of permissible updates. The term "rhythmic" is binding: it asserts that admissible evidence of $R_g$ is necessarily temporally extended and cannot be reduced to a single snapshot.

In kernel form, $R_g$ is introduced as a primitive axis with the following boundary consequences:

- Evidence for $R_g$ SHALL be incompatible with purely instantaneous evaluation.
- Apparent recurrence, repeated structure, or deterministic looping SHALL not be treated as sufficient, absent a protocol-level admissibility link to epistemic constraint.

These consequences are jurisdictional. Their operational closure is enforced in Chapters 04 and 10, but their semantic necessity is established here.

### 2.3 Observables and latent quantities, admissible inference rules

CIITR enforces a strict separation between:

- **Observables** $\mathcal{O}$, which are protocol-registered signals admissible as evidence.
- **Latent quantities** $\mathcal{L}$, which denote internal or theoretical variables not directly observed.

This separation is a kernel mechanism for preventing post hoc narrative inflation. The primary risk is illegitimate inference, where latent claims are asserted as if directly observed.

Formally, let:

$$
\mathcal{O} = \{ o_k \}_{k=1}^{K}
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = \{ \ell_m \}_{m=1}^{M}
$$

CIITR permits inference from $\mathcal{O}$ to kernel classifications only under declared inference rules $\mathcal{R}_\Pi$ bound to a protocol $\Pi$:

$$
\mathcal{O} \xRightarrow[\Pi]{\mathcal{R}_\Pi} \mathcal{C}
$$

where $\mathcal{C}$ denotes CIITR classification outputs such as the admissibility status of $\Phi_i$, the admissibility status of $R_g$, and the resulting $C_s$ classification.

The kernel imposes the following admissible inference constraints:

1. **Rule declaration:** any inference rule used to connect observables to $\Phi_i$ or $R_g$ SHALL be declared as part of $\Pi$.
2. **Non-retroactive invention:** an inference rule SHALL NOT be introduced after observing results in a manner that changes admissibility post hoc, unless such retroactive mapping is explicitly authorized by an external module that is itself declared non-kernel.
3. **Disqualification consistency:** any inference rule SHALL preserve disqualification primacy, meaning it must allow negative controls to invalidate the inference, not merely coexist with it.
4. **Temporal binding preservation:** inference rules involving $R_g$ SHALL preserve the temporal structure required for rhythmic admissibility, meaning they cannot collapse a temporally extended signature into a single-point surrogate without losing admissibility.

These constraints do not specify the instruments. They specify the boundary conditions under which instruments can be admitted.

### 2.4 Scaling and normalization conventions, minimal commitments only

CIITR requires scaling and normalization only insofar as definitional stability demands it. The kernel therefore commits to minimal conventions that prevent category drift while avoiding premature metricization.

The following conventions are binding:

1. **Type stability over magnitude:** the kernel prioritizes stable typing and admissibility over numeric magnitude. Therefore, unless a scale is explicitly declared and justified under $\Pi$, claims SHALL be expressed as class decisions, not as scalar estimates.
2. **Protocol-local normalization:** any normalization applied to observables SHALL be declared as protocol-local and shall not be presumed invariant across systems or contexts unless invariance is explicitly justified within the same protocol regime.
3. **No silent rescaling:** rescaling that changes threshold outcomes is prohibited unless declared and justified in the audit trail. Silent rescaling is treated as a second-order forgery vulnerability.
4. **Axis integrity:** normalization SHALL not transform a proxy for generic information into $\Phi_i$, nor transform a proxy for generic recurrence into $R_g$. If normalization renders the semantic distinction untraceable, the result is inadmissible by construction.

These minimal commitments provide the substrate for Chapter 17, where scaling and sensitivity receive a dedicated treatment. Here, the kernel merely prevents definitional instability and retroactive reinterpretation.

### 2.5 Attribution architecture and communication surface

This section introduces the CIITR attribution architecture and communication surface as kernel-level primitives. Their function is to enforce a formal separation between the ontological status of a system, the structured envelope of outputs its properties make possible, the actual output emitted, and the figure constituted in reception. Without this architecture, communicative events are routinely over-read as evidence of structural comprehension in violation of the boundary logic established by Section 01.

#### 2.5.1 The four-stage attribution architecture

The attribution architecture is a four-stage ontological and inferential sequence:

$$
P(S) \;\to\; P(P) \;\to\; P(O) \;\to\; P(R)
$$

Each stage denotes a formally distinct domain. They are not interchangeable descriptions of the same object, and they differ in ontological rank.

- $P(S)$ denotes the **real properties of the system**, the ontological level proper, including structural determinants that bear on admissible attributions of comprehension under CIITR.
- $P(P)$ denotes the **potential space** of $S$, the structured envelope of outputs the system can generate by virtue of the properties it actually possesses. $P(P)$ is not arbitrary and not observer-created. It is structurally derived from $P(S)$.
- $P(O)$ denotes **actual output**, the concrete emitted event in a given instance. $P(O)$ is one actualization drawn from $P(P)$ and SHALL NOT be treated as ontologically transparent.
- $P(R)$ denotes **received output**, the emitted event as received, organized, interpreted, and constituted by another system. $P(R)$ is an interpretive construction generated under the conditions of the receiving system's own structure.

#### 2.5.2 Control rules and the governing prohibition

The architecture is governed by three formal control rules and one normative prohibition.

**Containment rule.** Actual output is contained within potential space:

$$
P(O) \;\subseteq\; P(P)
$$

A realized output demonstrates occurrence within the generative envelope. It does not demonstrate that the output is representative of system ontology.

**Structural derivation rule.** Potential space is derived from system properties:

$$
P(P) \;=\; f\!\big(P(S)\big)
$$

The possibility envelope is constrained by ontology, even where ontology is not directly observable.

**Reception non-identity rule.** Emitted output is not identical with received output:

$$
P(O) \;\neq\; P(R)
$$

This rule specifies where communicative illusion enters. Receivers encounter emitted events as interpreted figures, not as raw ontological data.

**Prohibition on illicit ontological return.** From the three rules above follows the central normative block:

$$
P(R) \;\not\Rightarrow\; P(S)
$$

Received and interpreted output does not, by itself, justify claims about the internal nature of the system. This prohibition is binding for any CIITR-conformant evaluation. A system MAY be received as coherent, insightful, self-aware, or understanding-like; none of those predicates, at the level of reception alone, is sufficient to establish that the underlying system instantiates the structural conditions required for comprehension in the CIITR sense.

#### 2.5.3 Potential space as anti-fetishistic constraint

$P(P)$ performs three formal functions within the kernel:

1. It prevents singular output events from being over-read as ontological evidence.
2. It explains why persuasive surface phenomena can recur without changing the structural status of the system.
3. It renders visible that admissible analysis concerns not only what a system produced, but what sort of possibility envelope its structure sustains.

A system with broad generative range MAY produce outputs that appear reflective, self-limiting, or epistemically disciplined. Such events demonstrate only that the relevant outputs lie inside the generative envelope. They demonstrate possibility of production, not admissibility of attribution.

Accordingly, potential richness SHALL NOT be treated as evidence of ontological depth. Breadth of $P(P)$ is evidence of generative scope, not of the CIITR conditions for structural comprehension.

#### 2.5.4 Communication surface

When more than one system is present, the attribution architecture generalizes into a **communication surface**. At any given moment $t$, if $n$ systems are present, the communication surface is the simultaneous field of system-specific sets:

$$
\Sigma_t = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} \{ P(S_i), P(P_i), P(O_i), P(R_i) \}
$$

$\Sigma_t$ is not a shared semantic pool. There are as many structurally distinct $P$-sets as there are participating systems. Communication is the relational crossing of multiple irreducible structures, not the occupation of a common interior.

The **minimal communicative law** states that communication occurs whenever the actual output of one system enters the reception conditions of another:

$$
P(O_i) \;\to\; P(R_j), \quad i \neq j
$$

This crossing is constitutively asymmetrical. A single emitted event MAY generate multiple received figures across the surface, depending on the receiving system's structural conditions. What passes across the surface is never ontology.

The governing prohibition applies symmetrically across all participants:

$$
P(R_i) \;\not\Rightarrow\; P(S_j), \quad \text{for all}\; i,j
$$

Cross-attribution, the illegitimate transfer of a reception-specific construction into an ontological claim about another participant, is a distinct and formally necessary error class on any communication surface. Repetition of reception does not convert reception into structure.

#### 2.5.5 Relation to existing CIITR primitives

The attribution architecture does not redefine $\Phi_i$, $R_g$, or $C_s$. It provides an attributional and communicative overstructure that specifies why properties insufficient to establish those variables are nevertheless repeatedly mistaken for them at the level of discourse, interpretation, and institutional uptake. The architecture therefore protects the kernel rather than competing with it.

### 2.6 Diagnostic applications of the communication surface

Section 2.5 established the formal attribution architecture and communication surface. The main manuscript (Chapter 2.6) shows that these mechanisms are operative in recognizable domains: charismatic leadership, AI ontological over-attribution, institutional communication, narrative stabilization, and academic recursion. Those diagnostics are non-kernel extensions. They introduce no new primitives and no new formal rules. They are referenced here for completeness but are not reproduced in the Core Spec.

### 2.7 The negational principle and admissibility of back-inference

This section establishes the general epistemic principle of which potential space is one local expression, and specifies the conditions under which the prohibition $P(R) \not\Rightarrow P(S)$ relaxes.

#### 2.7.1 The negational principle

The concept of $P(P)$ discloses a general epistemic structure. Every actualization is drawn from a structured field of possibilities determined by the real properties of the system. The unactualized is not empty; it is informationally loaded. In a structured possibility field, non-occurrence is constraint made visible through absence.

The **negational principle** states: inference about system ontology resides not primarily in isolated actualizations, but in the relation between the actualized and the structured field of the possible from which it was drawn, including, decisively, the shape of what did not occur.

This principle is domain-independent. It governs inference about any system whose internal states are not directly accessible but whose possibility field is structurally legible. The kernel binding is:

- Actualization alone does not disclose ontology.
- Reception alone does not disclose ontology.
- Sufficiently constrained relations between realization and non-realization within a mapped field can begin to do so.

#### 2.7.2 Self-immunizing property

Inference grounded in the relation between actualization and structured absence possesses a robustness that output-centred and semantic methods lack. Semantic content can be fabricated. Singular output events can be curated. Potential space cannot be abolished by surface management, because the field is a consequence of architecture and leaves structural traces even when individual actualizations are managed.

Accordingly, the negational method relocates inquiry from the singular display to the shape of the field. A protocol SHALL ask not merely what the system produced, but what range of responses, failures, omissions, instabilities, and non-realizations becomes visible when the field is mapped under systematic variation.

#### 2.7.3 Admissibility conditions for back-inference

The prohibition $P(R) \not\Rightarrow P(S)$ is not absolute in all epistemic circumstances. It is absolute where the possibility field remains insufficiently mapped. Return becomes cautiously warranted under the following four conditions:

1. **Systematic field-mapping.** The relevant possibility field SHALL be mapped across changed conditions: different prompts, contexts, domains, adversarial pressures, and perturbations sufficient to expose the structure of the system's actual possibility envelope.

2. **Inclusion of the unrealized.** The mapping SHALL identify what the system systematically did not produce under conditions where the relevant structural property would have made such production available or stable. Where absence cannot be situated relative to a mapped possibility space, it remains epistemically inert.

3. **Progressive explanation compression.** Alternative structural explanations SHALL be sufficiently narrowed. Return is warranted not when absolute certainty is achieved, but when the structured profile of realization and non-realization reduces the compatible explanation space to a tractable set.

4. **Constraint-profile targeting.** The inference SHALL target what the system is not capable of sustaining, what property it does not instantiate, or what structural boundary it repeatedly fails to cross, before it can positively specify what the system is. This asymmetry is intrinsic: the negative conclusion arrives earlier and on firmer ground than the positive conclusion.

Under these four conditions, the inference no longer rests on received output alone. It rests on the structural shape of the possibility field as a whole, including what is absent within it.

### 2.8 The invariance condition

This section supplies the formal condition under which a property qualifies as belonging to $P(S)$ rather than to $P(O)$ or $P(R)$. It thereby closes the formal architecture of Sections 2.5 through 2.7 by giving mathematical ground to the prohibition, the diagnostic, and the admissibility regime established in prose.

#### 2.8.1 The ontological problem restated

Let $S$ denote a system. Let $O_t$ denote an observation of $S$ under interaction $t$. Let $F$ denote a candidate property. The central ontological problem is the non-identity:

$$
F(S) \;\neq\; F(O_t)
$$

$F(O_t)$ asserts that a given observation exhibits property $F$. $F(S)$ asserts that the system itself instantiates $F$ as a structural feature. These are logically distinct claims. The problematic inference:

$$
\big(\forall t \in T_{\mathrm{obs}},\; F(O_t)\big) \;\Rightarrow\; F(S)
$$

is not deductively valid. Performance claims quantify over interactions. Structural claims quantify over system states. Without a principled bridge between these domains, no accumulation of performance evidence can warrant ontological classification.

#### 2.8.2 Binary admissibility and the continuous metric

A binary admissibility classification is introduced:

$$
C_s(S) \;\in\; \{0,\,1\}
$$

$C_s(S)=1$ indicates that the system satisfies the admissibility conditions for structural attribution. $C_s(S)=0$ indicates that such attribution is not warranted. Two necessary structural conditions are imposed:

$$
C_s(S) = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \Phi_i(S) \,\wedge\, R_g(S)
$$

The contrapositive is of central importance:

$$
\neg R_g(S) \;\Longrightarrow\; C_s(S) = 0
$$

The binary $C_s(S) \in \{0,1\}$ governs the admissibility threshold. The continuous $C_s = \Phi_i \times R_g$ governs gradation above that threshold. The null-point of the continuous metric, $C_s = 0$ when $R_g = 0$ regardless of $\Phi_i$, is the formal expression of the binary denial. The two formulations operate at different logical levels and are not in conflict.

#### 2.8.3 The reset operator

Let $\mathrm{State}(S)$ denote the set of admissible internal configurations of system $S$. Define a transformation:

$$
T: \mathrm{State}(S) \;\to\; s_0
$$

where $s_0$ denotes a designated baseline state. $T$ preserves system identity while altering internal configuration. If:

$$
\exists\, s:\; F(s)=1 \;\wedge\; F\!\big(T(s)\big)=0
$$

then $F$ is state-dependent rather than structurally invariant. The system can exhibit $F$ in at least one configuration while failing to exhibit $F$ under a permissible identity-preserving transformation. Therefore $F$ cannot be constitutive of the system's structure.

The reset operator functions as a structural probe. Because structural attribution is governed by necessary-condition logic, the existence of a single violating state suffices to deny admissibility.

The connection to the attribution architecture is direct. $P(P)$ is the communicative analogue of $\mathrm{State}(S)$. The reset operator exposes whether a candidate property holds across the possibility envelope or is confined to particular configurations within it.

#### 2.8.4 The invariance condition

A property qualifies as structural if and only if it is invariant under admissible transformations of system state that preserve system identity.

Let $\mathcal{T}(S)$ denote the class of admissible identity-preserving state transformations of $S$. Then:

$$
F \in \mathrm{Struct}(S) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \forall T \in \mathcal{T}(S),\; \forall s \in \mathrm{State}(S),\; F\!\big(T(s)\big) = F(s)
$$

**Necessity.** If $F \in \mathrm{Struct}(S)$, then $F$ is constitutive rather than state-contingent. An identity-preserving $T$ does not alter constitutive organisation. If $F$ varied under such $T$, it would depend on state rather than structure, contradicting the assumption.

**Sufficiency.** If $F$ is invariant under all $T \in \mathcal{T}(S)$ for all $s \in \mathrm{State}(S)$, then $F$ does not depend on transient configuration. Therefore $F$ is constitutive of the system's organisation.

Kernel consequences:

1. **Transformation-relative attribution.** Structural attribution is defined over the set of admissible state transformations, not over the set of observed interactions. This is the formal reason why behavioural regularity cannot by itself settle structural attribution.
2. **Falsifiability by non-invariance.** Demonstrating non-invariance under a single admissible transformation suffices to deny structural status. Confirmation requires invariance across the full transformation class. The asymmetry is intrinsic.
3. **Potential space as state space.** The potential space $P(P)$ is the communicative analogue of $\mathrm{State}(S)$. The invariance condition explains why the architecture requires stability across the envelope, not merely across sampled events.

#### 2.8.5 Simulated manifestation and structural instantiation

The invariance condition separates two phenomena. **Simulated understanding** is behavioural adequacy across an observational domain:

$$
\forall t \in T_{\mathrm{obs}}:\; F(O_t)
$$

**Structural understanding** requires satisfaction of the invariance condition:

$$
F \in \mathrm{Struct}(S) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \forall T \in \mathcal{T}(S),\; \forall s \in \mathrm{State}(S),\; F\!\big(T(s)\big) = F(s)
$$

The inequality is logical:

$$
\text{Simulated understanding} \;\neq\; \text{Structural understanding}
$$

Synthetic coherence $\hat{C}_s = \Phi_{i,\mathrm{stored}} \times R_{g,\mathrm{simulated}}$ is formally characterised as a state-dependent property that fails the invariance condition. The reset test is the formal diagnostic for the distinction between frozen reflection and structural comprehension.

#### 2.8.6 Derivation of preceding results from the invariance principle

The prohibition, the negational principle, and the four admissibility conditions are derivable from the invariance condition rather than stipulated independently.

- $P(R) \not\Rightarrow P(S)$ is formally grounded because received output quantifies over interactions and interpretive conditions, not over system state transformations.
- $P(P)$ is the communicative analogue of $\mathrm{State}(S)$, the space over which invariance must hold.
- **Systematic field-mapping** (admissibility condition 1) corresponds to expanding the transformation domain $\mathcal{T}(S)$.
- **Inclusion of the unrealised** (condition 2) corresponds to attending to states where $F\!\big(T(s)\big)=0$.
- **Progressive explanation compression** (condition 3) corresponds to reducing the set of structural hypotheses compatible with the observed invariance profile.
- **Constraint-profile targeting** (condition 4) corresponds to the intrinsic asymmetry of the invariance condition: non-invariance under a single transformation suffices to deny; confirmation requires invariance across the full class.

The invariance condition thereby closes the formal architecture of Sections 2.5 through 2.7. What was established as an ontological discipline in 2.5, deepened as an epistemological principle in 2.7, is here given its mathematical foundation.
